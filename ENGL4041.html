1. Language Varieties

1.1 Distinctness of Human Language

Human language is unique among animal communication systems. Key design features (after Charles Hockett) include:

  • Duality of patterning – meaningless sounds (phonemes) combine into meaningful units (morphemes/words).
  • Productivity / Creativity – infinite novel sentences from finite rules.
  • Displacement – talk about things not present in time or space.
  • Arbitrariness – no necessary link between form and meaning (except onomatopoeia).
  • Cultural transmission – language is learned, not genetically fixed.
  • Reflexivity – language can be used to talk about language itself.

Animal systems are largely stimulus-bound, closed, and lack true productivity and displacement at the level of human language.

1.2 Dialects, Idiolects and Isogloss

Dialect

A variety of a language associated with a particular region or social group. Differs in pronunciation (accent), vocabulary and grammar. Example: British English vs Indian English; Cockney vs RP.

Idiolect

The unique language variety of an individual speaker – personal vocabulary preferences, pronunciation quirks, favourite syntactic patterns. Everyone has an idiolect.

Isogloss

A geographic boundary line marking the limit of a particular linguistic feature (e.g., the line separating “bucket” vs “pail” usage areas in the USA).

Dialect continua exist: neighbouring dialects are mutually intelligible, but distant ones may not be. Standard languages are often socially privileged dialects.

1.3 Verbal and Non-Verbal Language

Verbal language uses words (spoken or written). Non-verbal language includes body language, gestures, facial expressions, posture, proxemics (space), haptics (touch), paralanguage (tone, pitch, volume, speed), and silence.

Key Insight:

Research (Mehrabian and others) often cited suggests that in emotional communication a large proportion of meaning is carried by non-verbal cues. However, the exact percentages are context-dependent. In academic or technical discourse, verbal content dominates; in interpersonal conflict, non-verbal signals become critical.

Both modes work together. A sarcastic “Nice job!” is understood through tone and facial expression contradicting the words.

1.5 Gender-Sensitivity of Language

Language both reflects and shapes social attitudes toward gender. Traditional English has been criticised for:

  • Generic “he” / “man” (mankind, chairman, fireman)
  • Asymmetrical terms (master/mistress, bachelor/spinster)
  • Lexical gaps or markedness (male as default)

Gender-sensitive alternatives: humankind, chairperson/chair, firefighter, Ms, singular “they”, “he or she”, or rephrasing to plural. Many institutions now adopt inclusive language guidelines. Awareness of gendered metaphors and stereotypes in discourse is also part of linguistic sensitivity.

1.6 New-Age Language: Journalism, Social Media & Internet

Journalism

Inverted pyramid, headlinese (omission of articles/verbs), loaded language, soundbites, clickbait, neutrality claims vs framing.

Social Media

Character limits → abbreviations, emoji as paralanguage, hashtags as topic markers, memes as multimodal texts, performative identity.

Internet / Digital

Netspeak, LOLspeak, reaction GIFs, multimodal discourse, rapid lexical innovation (ghosting, adulting, rizz), code-switching across platforms.

Digital language is characterised by multimodality, speed, ephemerality, and the blending of spoken and written features (e.g., “gonna”, ellipsis, capitalisation for emphasis).

2. Language and Emotion

2.1 Plain Language vs Figurative Language

Plain (literal) language aims at clarity, directness and denotation. It is preferred in science, law, technical writing and instructions.

Figurative language uses figures of speech to create imagery, emotional resonance, emphasis or aesthetic pleasure. It dominates poetry, advertising, rhetoric and everyday creative talk.

Both have value. Over-figurative academic writing can obscure meaning; purely plain creative writing may lack impact.

2.2 Figures of Speech (Detailed)

Simile

Explicit comparison using like or as.

“My love is like a red, red rose.” (Burns)

Creates vivid, accessible imagery.

Metaphor

Implicit comparison – one thing is another.

“All the world’s a stage.” (Shakespeare)

More compressed and powerful than simile.

Epigram

Concise, witty, often paradoxical statement.

“I can resist everything except temptation.” (Oscar Wilde)

Memorable and pointed.

Oxymoron

Juxtaposition of contradictory terms.

“sweet sorrow”, “living death”, “deafening silence”

Highlights complexity or tension.

Irony

Meaning opposite to (or sharply different from) the literal sense. Types: verbal, situational, dramatic.

“What a pleasant day!” (said during a storm)

Innuendo

Indirect suggestion, often of something discreditable or sexual; implication rather than open statement.

“He is a man of great experience… in certain matters.”

Pun

Play on words exploiting multiple meanings or similar sounds.

“A boiled egg every morning is hard to beat.”

Alliteration

Repetition of initial consonant sounds.

“Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers.”

Creates rhythm and memorability.

Hyperbole

Deliberate exaggeration for emphasis or effect.

“I’ve told you a million times!”

Hyperbaton

Unusual or inverted word order for emphasis or poetic effect.

“This I must see.” / “Object there was none. Passion there was none.” (Poe)

Assonance

Repetition of vowel sounds within nearby words (often creating internal rhyme or musicality).

“The rain in Spain stays mainly in the plain.” / “Hear the mellow wedding bells.” (Poe)

Why Figures of Speech Matter for Creativity

They allow speakers and writers to compress complex ideas, evoke emotion, create aesthetic pleasure, and persuade. Mastery of figures is central to rhetorical skill and literary creativity.

3. Register and Collocation

3.1 What is Register?

Register is a variety of language defined by its use in a particular social setting or for a particular purpose. It is determined by three main variables (Halliday):

  • Field – what is happening / subject matter
  • Tenor – who is taking part / relationships
  • Mode – channel (spoken/written, monologue/dialogue, etc.)

Examples of registers:

Legal register Medical register Academic register Journalistic register Religious/liturgical Sports commentary Advertising Classroom talk

Each register has characteristic vocabulary (technical terms), grammatical preferences, and discourse structure.

3.2 Collocation

Collocation is the tendency of certain words to co-occur more frequently than would be expected by chance. J.R. Firth: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.”

Strong Collocations

Highly predictable; limited choice of partners.

  • blonde hair (not *blonde car)
  • commit a crime / suicide
  • make a decision / mistake
  • heavy rain / traffic / smoker
  • rancid butter / oil
  • torrential rain

Weak / Open Collocations

More flexible; many possible partners.

  • good / nice / lovely day
  • big / large / huge problem
  • do / make / carry out research
  • important / significant / major issue

Collocational Violation

Using a word combination that is grammatically possible but collocationally unnatural or incorrect for native speakers.

Examples of violation:

  • *strong rain → heavy rain
  • *make a photo → take a photo
  • *do a decision → make a decision
  • *powerful tea → strong tea
  • *commit a mistake → make a mistake / commit an error

Awareness of collocation is essential for natural, fluent and creative language use. Learners often produce grammatically correct but collocationally odd sentences.

4. Understanding Ambiguity

Ambiguity occurs when a linguistic expression can be interpreted in more than one way. It is a central feature of language that can be exploited creatively (puns, poetry, advertising) or cause misunderstanding.

Lexical Ambiguity

Arises from words having multiple meanings (polysemy or homonymy)

“The bank was closed.”

Financial institution or river bank?

“She can’t bear children.”

Tolerate them or give birth to them?

“He saw the bark.”

Tree covering or dog’s sound?

“They are flying planes.”

Planes that fly or people who fly planes?

Syntactic Ambiguity

Arises from different possible grammatical structures

“Visiting relatives can be boring.”

Relatives who visit or the act of visiting relatives?

“The chicken is ready to eat.”

Chicken is cooked or chicken wants to eat?

“I saw the man with the telescope.”

Man holding telescope or I used a telescope?

“Old men and women”

[Old men] and women or old [men and women]?

Creative Use of Ambiguity

Writers and speakers deliberately use ambiguity for humour (puns), depth (poetry), or persuasion (advertising slogans). In everyday communication, context, prosody and shared knowledge usually resolve ambiguity. In legal and technical writing, ambiguity is carefully avoided.

5.2 Airstream Mechanism

The airstream mechanism is the way air is set in motion for speech. There are three main types used in the world’s languages:

Mechanism Initiator Direction English? Examples elsewhere
PulmonicLungs + diaphragmEgressive (out)Yes – all soundsAlmost all languages
GlottalicLarynx (glottis)Egressive (ejectives) / Ingressive (implosives)No (rare paralinguistic)Amharic, Quechua, many African languages
Velaric (Lingual)Tongue + velumIngressive (clicks)No (only “tut-tut” etc.)Khoisan, Zulu, Xhosa

English uses exclusively the pulmonic egressive airstream – air is pushed out from the lungs. All 44 phonemes are produced this way.

5.4 Three-Term Description

Every consonant can be uniquely identified by a three-term label:

  1. Voicing – voiced (+) or voiceless (−)
  2. Place of articulation – where in the vocal tract the obstruction occurs (bilabial, labiodental, dental, alveolar, post-alveolar, palato-alveolar, palatal, velar, glottal…)
  3. Manner of articulation – how the airstream is obstructed (plosive/stop, affricate, fricative, nasal, approximant, lateral…)

Examples:

  • /p/ = voiceless bilabial plosive
  • /d/ = voiced alveolar plosive
  • /ʃ/ = voiceless palato-alveolar fricative
  • /m/ = voiced bilabial nasal
  • /l/ = voiced alveolar lateral approximant

Vowels are described by a different set of parameters (often more than three):

  • Height of the tongue (close / high, mid, open / low)
  • Backness (front, central, back)
  • Lip rounding (rounded / unrounded)
  • Length (long / short) and tenseness are also relevant in English.

Example: /iː/ = close front unrounded long vowel; /ɒ/ = open back rounded short vowel.
