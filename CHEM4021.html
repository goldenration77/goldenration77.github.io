💧 Liquid State

6 Hours · Physical Chemistry

📌 Surface Tension

Definition: Force acting per unit length perpendicular to an imaginary line drawn on the liquid surface. Tends to minimize surface area.
γ = F / l    SI unit: N m⁻¹    CGS: dyne cm⁻¹
Dimension: [M T⁻²]

Principle (Stalagmometer): Drop-weight / drop-number method. Number of drops from equal volumes of two liquids is inversely proportional to their surface tensions (approx.).

γ₁ / γ₂ = (n₂ / n₁) × (d₁ / d₂)    (n = no. of drops, d = density)

📌 Viscosity

Definition: Internal resistance offered by a liquid to the flow of one layer over another. Coefficient of viscosity (η) is the tangential force required to maintain unit velocity gradient between two parallel layers of unit area.
η = F / [A · (dv/dx)]    Unit: poise (CGS) or Pa·s (SI)
1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s

Ostwald Viscometer: Time of flow of fixed volume through a capillary under gravity.

η₁ / η₂ = (t₁ / t₂) × (d₁ / d₂)

🌡️ Temperature Effect (Qualitative)

  • Surface Tension ↓ with ↑ temperature (kinetic energy increases → intermolecular forces weaken).
  • Viscosity ↓ with ↑ temperature for liquids (molecules move more freely).
  • For gases, viscosity increases with temperature.
Quick Tip: At critical temperature, surface tension becomes zero (no distinction between liquid & vapour).
CHEATSHEET
• Higher intermolecular force → higher γ & η
• Stalagmometer → surface tension
• Ostwald → viscosity
• Both decrease with temperature for pure liquids

⚖️ Colligative Properties

6 Hours · Physical Chemistry
Properties that depend only on the number of solute particles, not on their nature.

1. Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure (Raoult’s Law)

(P⁰ − Pₛ) / P⁰ = x₂    (x₂ = mole fraction of solute)
For dilute solutions: (P⁰ − Pₛ) / P⁰ ≈ n₂ / n₁

2. Elevation of Boiling Point

ΔTb = Kb · m    (m = molality)
Kb = RTb² / (1000 · ΔHvap)    (ebullioscopic constant)

3. Depression of Freezing Point

ΔTf = Kf · m
Kf = RTf² / (1000 · ΔHfus)    (cryoscopic constant)

4. Osmosis & Osmotic Pressure

Spontaneous flow of solvent through a semi-permeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution.
π = CRT    (van’t Hoff)    for dilute solutions
πV = nRT    (like ideal gas)

Isotonic: same π. Hypertonic / Hypotonic relative terms.

Abnormal Colligative Properties & van’t Hoff Factor

i = Observed colligative property / Calculated colligative property
i = Observed molar mass / Calculated molar mass (inverse for association)
  • Dissociation (electrolytes): i > 1   e.g. NaCl → i ≈ 2
  • Association (e.g. acetic acid in benzene): i < 1
Degree of dissociation α = (i − 1) / (n − 1)
Degree of association α = (1 − i) / (1 − 1/n)

Molecular Weight Determination

From any colligative property: measure ΔTb, ΔTf, π or relative lowering → calculate M of unknown solute.

CHEATSHEET – Order of magnitude
ΔTb / ΔTf ∝ m  |  π ∝ C  |  Relative lowering ∝ x₂
Useful for non-volatile solute + volatile solvent.
Osmotic pressure is most accurate for high MW (polymers).

🧪 Solutions

8 Hours · Physical Chemistry

Ideal Solutions & Raoult’s Law

Ideal solution: obeys Raoult’s law over entire composition range.
PA = PA⁰ · xA  ;  PB = PB⁰ · xB
ΔHmix = 0, ΔVmix = 0, intermolecular forces A–A ≈ B–B ≈ A–B.

Non-Ideal Solutions – Deviations

  • Positive deviation: Ptotal > expected (A–B weaker). Example: ethanol + water, acetone + CS₂. Forms minimum boiling azeotrope.
  • Negative deviation: Ptotal < expected (A–B stronger). Example: chloroform + acetone, HNO₃ + water. Forms maximum boiling azeotrope.

Vapour Pressure & Temperature Composition Curves

Ideal: linear VP vs composition. Non-ideal: curved. Boiling point diagrams show liquid + vapour curves; tie-line used with Lever rule.

Lever Rule

nliquid / nvapour = (xoverall − y) / (x − xoverall)
(lengths of tie-line segments inverse to amounts)

Azeotropes

Constant boiling mixtures that distill without change in composition. Cannot be separated by simple fractional distillation.
  • Minimum boiling (positive deviation) e.g. 95.6% ethanol–water
  • Maximum boiling (negative deviation) e.g. 68% HNO₃–water

Critical Solution Temperature (CST)

Temperature above (UCST) or below (LCST) which two partially miscible liquids become completely miscible.

  • Phenol–water: UCST ≈ 66 °C
  • Triethylamine–water: LCST
  • Nicotine–water: both UCST & LCST

Impurity usually raises UCST / lowers LCST (affects mutual solubility).

Immiscible Liquids – Steam Distillation

Used for purifying organic liquids immiscible with water that decompose at their normal BP. Mixture boils when sum of vapour pressures equals atmospheric pressure → lower temperature.
P_total = P_org + P_water = 1 atm

Nernst Distribution Law

C₁ / C₂ = KD    (at constant T, for dilute solutions)
If solute associates/dissociates → modified form.

Applications: Solvent extraction (multiple extractions better than one), determination of association, purification.

CHEATSHEET
Ideal → no ΔH/ΔV · Positive → max VP, min BP azeotrope
Negative → min VP, max BP azeotrope
Steam distillation → immiscible + lower T
Nernst → solvent extraction efficiency

⬡ Aromatic Hydrocarbons

6 Hours · Organic Chemistry

Benzene – Preparations

  • From phenol: C₆H₅OH + Zn dust → C₆H₆ + ZnO (distillation)
  • Decarboxylation: C₆H₅COONa + NaOH (CaO) → C₆H₆ + Na₂CO₃
  • From acetylene: 3 HC≡CH → C₆H₆ (red-hot Cu tube, 500 °C)
  • From benzene sulphonic acid: C₆H₅SO₃H + H₂O → C₆H₆ + H₂SO₄ (superheated steam / dilute acid)

Electrophilic Substitution – General Mechanism

π-electrons attack electrophile E⁺ → σ-complex (arenium ion) → loss of H⁺ → substituted benzene. Rate-determining step is formation of σ-complex.

Key Reactions (up to 4C side chain)

ReactionReagentsProduct
Nitrationconc. HNO₃ + H₂SO₄Nitrobenzene
ChlorinationCl₂ / FeCl₃ (or AlCl₃)Chlorobenzene
BrominationBr₂ / FeBr₃Bromobenzene
Sulphonationconc. / fuming H₂SO₄Benzene sulphonic acid
FC AlkylationRCl / AlCl₃ (R ≤ 4C)Alkylbenzene
FC AcylationRCOCl / AlCl₃Acylbenzene (ketone)

Nitration Mechanism (detailed)

HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ ⇌ NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻
Benzene + NO₂⁺ → σ-complex → Nitrobenzene + H⁺

Side-chain Oxidation

Alkylbenzenes (any length, but ≤4C here) → benzoic acid with alkaline / acidic KMnO₄ (or K₂Cr₂O₇). Side chain must have at least one benzylic H.

C₆H₅–CH₂R --[KMnO₄]→ C₆H₅–COOH
Note: FC alkylation can rearrange (carbocation); acylation does not. Polyalkylation possible; acylation stops after one.
CHEATSHEET
Prep: phenol/Zn · Na-benzoate/soda-lime · acetylene · sulphonic acid
EAS: NO₂⁺, X₂/FeX₃, SO₃, R⁺/RCO⁺ with AlCl₃
Side-chain ox → always –COOH if benzylic H present

🔧 Grignard & Active Methylene

6 Hours · Organic Chemistry

Grignard Reagent (RMgX)

Prepared from alkyl/aryl halide + Mg in dry ether. Highly polar Cδ⁻–Mgδ⁺. Acts as strong nucleophile & base. Moisture sensitive.

Synthetic Uses of GR

  • With aldehydes: RMgX + R′CHO → secondary alcohol (HCHO → primary)
  • With ketones: → tertiary alcohol
  • With CO₂: → carboxylic acid (after hydrolysis)
  • With esters: → tertiary alcohol (two equivalents)
  • With epoxides: → primary alcohol (longer chain)
  • With H₂O / ROH / acid: → hydrocarbon (RH)

Ethyl Acetoacetate (EAA) – Active Methylene

CH₃COCH₂COOC₂H₅. Methylene group flanked by two carbonyls → acidic H (pKa ≈ 11). Forms stable enolate with base (NaOEt).

Acetoacetic Ester Synthesis:

  1. Generate enolate (NaOEt)
  2. Alkylate with R–X (SN2)
  3. Can dialkylate
  4. Ketonic hydrolysis (dil. acid / heat) → methyl ketone RCH₂COCH₃
  5. Acid hydrolysis (conc. alkali then acid) → substituted acetic acid

Diethyl Malonate (DEM)

CH₂(COOC₂H₅)₂. Similar active methylene. Used for synthesis of substituted acetic acids / monocarboxylic acids.

Alkylation → hydrolysis → decarboxylation → R–CH₂COOH (or R₂CHCOOH).

Key Difference: EAA → ketones or acids; DEM → primarily substituted acetic acids. Both require anhydrous conditions for alkylation step.
CHEATSHEET
GR: RMgX + carbonyl → alcohol; + CO₂ → acid
EAA: enolate + RX → alkylated → ketone (ketonic hyd.) or acid
DEM: enolate + RX → alkylated → RCH₂COOH after hyd. + decarb.
Active methylene = acidic H between two EWG

🔗 Coordination Chemistry

10 Hours · Inorganic Chemistry

Double Salts vs Complex Salts

  • Double salt: exists only in solid; in solution gives all ions (e.g. Mohr’s salt).
  • Complex salt: retains identity in solution; complex ion does not fully dissociate (e.g. K₄[Fe(CN)₆]).

Werner’s Theory

Primary valency (ionizable, = oxidation state) satisfied by negative ions.
Secondary valency (non-ionizable, = coordination number) satisfied by ligands directed in space (geometry).

Ligands & Chelates

  • Monodentate: Cl⁻, NH₃, H₂O, CO, CN⁻
  • Bidentate: en, ox²⁻, bipy, acac⁻
  • Polydentate: EDTA⁴⁻ (hexadentate)
  • Chelate: ring-forming polydentate ligand complex → extra stability (chelate effect).

Coordination Number & Geometry

CN 2: linear · CN 4: tetrahedral or square planar · CN 6: octahedral (most common).

IUPAC Nomenclature (up to 2 metal centres)

  1. Cation first, then anion.
  2. Ligands alphabetical order (ignore prefixes di, tri…).
  3. Anionic ligands end in –o (chloro, cyano, oxalato…).
  4. Neutral ligands keep name (aqua, ammine, carbonyl…).
  5. Metal name + oxidation state in Roman numerals (in brackets).
  6. If complex is anion, metal ends in –ate.
Example: [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl → tetraamminedichloridocobalt(III) chloride
K₃[Fe(CN)₆] → potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III)

Isomerism

Constitutional (Structural):

  • Ionization: [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ vs [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br
  • Hydrate: [Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃ vs [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O
  • Linkage: –NO₂ (nitro) vs –ONO (nitrito); –SCN vs –NCS
  • Coordination: different ligands between two complexes
  • Coordination position (dinuclear)

Stereo isomerism:

  • Geometrical: cis–trans in square planar (MA₂B₂) & octahedral (MA₄B₂, MA₃B₃).
  • Optical: non-superimposable mirror images. Common in octahedral [M(AA)₃], cis-[M(AA)₂B₂]. Square planar rarely optically active.
CHEATSHEET
Werner: primary = OS, secondary = CN
Chelate effect → higher stability
Nomenclature: ligands α-betical, metal(OS), –ate if anionic
Geo: cis/trans · Optical: chiral octahedral especially with bidentate

☢️ Radioactivity

3 Hours · Inorganic / Nuclear

Characteristics

  • Spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei.
  • Independent of chemical state, temperature, pressure (nuclear phenomenon).
  • First-order kinetics.

Types of Rays

RayNatureChargeMassPenetrating power
αHe²⁺ nucleus+24 uLeast (stopped by paper)
βelectron (e⁻)−1~0Medium (Al sheet)
γEM radiation00Highest (Pb needed)

Radioactive Disintegration & Equilibrium

Parent → Daughter. Successive decays can reach secular or transient equilibrium when half-lives differ appropriately.

Decay Constant, Half-life, Average Life

Rate = λN    (λ = decay constant)
N = N₀ e−λt
t1/2 = ln2 / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ
Average life (τ) = 1 / λ = 1.44 × t1/2

Artificial Transmutation & Artificial Radioactivity

Stable nucleus bombarded by particles (α, n, p, d…) → new nucleus (may be radioactive). Example: ¹⁴N + α → ¹⁷O + p (Rutherford). Curie & Joliot: artificial radioactivity (e.g. ²⁷Al + α → ³⁰P + n; ³⁰P is positron emitter).

Uses

  • Carbon-14 dating
  • Medical: ⁶⁰Co (γ therapy), ¹³¹I (thyroid), ⁹⁹ᵐTc (imaging)
  • Tracers in chemical & biological studies
  • Power generation (nuclear reactors)
  • Sterilization, thickness gauges, smoke detectors (²⁴¹Am)
CHEATSHEET
α = He nucleus · β = e⁻ · γ = photon
t1/2 = 0.693/λ · τ = 1/λ
Artificial radioactivity: induced by bombardment
Applications: dating, medicine, tracers, energy
